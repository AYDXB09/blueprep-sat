Standard English Conventions — Agreement Q14

The collection of rare manuscripts, donated by an anonymous alum last spring, was✓ housed in the university's east wing.

subject ↔ verb ‌traps — click each

Subject–verb agreement

The dimmed phrase between "collection" and the blank is an interrupting modifier — it doesn't change the subject. "Collection" is singular, so the verb must be singular: was.

Full guide: interrupting phrases

Trap 1 — "manuscripts"

Plural noun, closest to the verb visually — the classic proximity trap. If you matched the verb to this instead of the real subject, you'd wrongly pick "were." It sits inside "of rare manuscripts," a prepositional phrase, so it can never be the subject.

Trap 2 — "anonymous alum"

A second false subject — and a sneaky one, because "alum" is singular, so treating it as the subject still happens to produce "was," the correct verb. Right answer, wrong reasoning: it's inside "donated by an anonymous alum," a modifying phrase, not the subject. Relying on this shortcut would fail on a sentence where the nearest noun is plural instead.

Answer choices

1wasCorrect — matches the singular subject "collection."
2wereWrong — agrees with "manuscripts" (Trap 1), not the real subject.
3have beenWrong — plural, same trap as "were," plus the wrong aspect for this sentence.
4areWrong — present-tense plural; clashes with singular "collection" and the past-tense "donated."
Standard English Conventions — Agreement R&W Q20

Researchers studying the "terra-cotta army" were shocked to realize that the shape of each statue's ears, like the shape of each person's ears, is✓ unique.

subject ↔ verb ‌traps — click each

Subject–verb agreement, across a comparison

"Like the shape of each person's ears" is a comparison set off by commas — it doesn't add a second subject, it just restates the same structure about a different noun. The real subject stays "the shape," singular, so the verb is is, not "are."

Full guide: comparative interrupters

Trap 1 — "ears" (first)

Plural, right next to a spot that feels like it should take a verb — but it's inside "of each statue's ears," a prepositional phrase. Can't be the subject.

Trap 2 — "ears" (second)

Easy to misread the whole "like the shape of each person's ears" clause as a second thing being described — as if the sentence were about two things, which would call for "are." It's a comparison, not a compound subject.

Answer choices

1isCorrect — matches the singular subject "the shape."
2areWrong — agrees with "ears" (either trap), not the real subject.
3wereWrong — same trap as "are," plus wrong tense for a present-tense sentence.
4have beenWrong — plural and the wrong aspect.
Standard English Conventions — Pronouns Q22

Before presenting to the board, each of the researchers revised his or her✓ section three times.

true antecedent ↔ pronoun ‌trap — click it

Pronoun–antecedent agreement

"Of the researchers" is a prepositional phrase modifying "each" — it's the trap, not the antecedent. Each is the grammatical head and is always singular, so the pronoun must match: his or her, not "their."

Full guide: indefinite pronouns

Trap — "researchers"

Plural, and it's the noun closest to the verb "revised" — the same proximity trap as "manuscripts" in Q14. But it's the object of "of," not the antecedent. The antecedent is "each," which governs the pronoun regardless of how many researchers there are.

Answer choices

1his or herCorrect — matches singular antecedent "each."
2theirWrong — treats "researchers," the trap, as the antecedent instead of "each."
3itsWrong — implies a non-person antecedent; each researcher is a person.
4ourWrong — shifts to first person; nothing in the sentence supports "our."
Standard English Conventions — Boundaries Q08

The bridge was closedfor repairs ;✓ commuters had to find alternate routes for nearly a month.

clause A — subject underlined, verb italic clause B — subject underlined, verb italic ‌trap — click it

Joining two independent clauses

Clause A has a subject ("The bridge") and a complete verb ("was closed") — "for repairs" just adds detail, it isn't required for the clause to stand alone. Clause B has its own subject ("commuters") and verb ("had to find") plus its own object and modifier. Because each side already has everything it needs to be its own sentence, joining them with a comma alone is a comma splice. The blank needs a semicolon, a period, or a comma + coordinating conjunction (and/but/so...) — something strong enough to hold two complete sentences together.

Full guide: comma splices & boundaries

Trap — "for repairs" feels unfinished

Sentences that trail off with a prepositional phrase can feel incomplete, which tempts students to underestimate clause A and reach for a lighter connector like a comma. But completeness is decided by subject + verb, not by how the sentence ends — "The bridge was closed" is already a full sentence; "for repairs" is optional detail sitting on top of it.

Answer choices

1;Correct — a semicolon joins two independent clauses without a conjunction.
2,Wrong — a comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice.
3, thusWrong — "thus" is a conjunctive adverb, not a coordinating conjunction; still a splice.
4beingWrong — turns the second clause into a dangling participial phrase, not a full sentence.
Standard English Conventions — Boundaries R&W Q — cathedral

Although the 1866 renovation preserved the cathedral's facade,✓ the interior was almost entirely rebuilt in a modern style.

clause A — dependent (subordinator up front) clause B — independent ‌trap — click it

Introductory dependent clause needs a comma

Clause A has its own subject ("the renovation") and verb ("preserved") — but it opens with "Although," a subordinating conjunction, which makes the whole clause dependent no matter how complete it looks internally. A dependent clause introducing an independent one is set off with a comma, not a semicolon (semicolons only join two independent clauses) and not a period (that would leave "Although..." as a sentence fragment).

Trap — subject + verb ≠ independent

This clause has a full subject and verb, which is exactly what makes semicolon feel plausible — the Q08 rule "both sides look complete → semicolon" almost applies. But "Although" is a subordinating conjunction: it turns a complete-looking clause into a dependent one. Subject + verb is necessary for independence, but a leading subordinator overrides it.

Answer choices

1,Correct — a comma sets off an introductory dependent clause from the independent clause that follows.
2;Wrong — a semicolon can only join two independent clauses; "Although..." isn't one.
3.Wrong — ending the sentence here leaves "Although the renovation preserved the facade" as a fragment.
4(no punctuation)Wrong — an introductory dependent clause needs a comma to separate it from the main clause.
Expression of Ideas — Transitions Q31

The novel received little attention upon its initial release. However✓, it is now considered a landmark of twentieth-century literature.

the two ideas being weighed against each other ‌trap — click it

Transition logic

"Little attention" and "landmark" are opposites — the second sentence contradicts the first, it doesn't build on it. That calls for a contrast transition: However, not "Therefore" or "Similarly," both of which would treat the two ideas as agreeing.

Full guide: contrast vs. cause vs. continuation

Trap — "now" isn't a cause

"Now" marks that time has passed, and time passing can feel like it explains a change — which is exactly what makes "Therefore" tempting. But the sentence never says why opinion changed, only that it did, and that it's the opposite of the original reception. Passage of time is not a stated cause.

Answer choices

1HoweverCorrect — signals the contrast between "little attention" and "landmark."
2ThereforeWrong — implies cause-and-effect; there's no causal link stated, only the passage of time.
3SimilarlyWrong — implies agreement between the two ideas, but they're opposites.
4In additionWrong — implies continuation, not the reversal the sentence describes.

Information and Ideas — Rhetorical Synthesis R&W Q25

Goal: refute the claim that malapportionment in the Storting favors small urban districts.

Student's notes

  • Malapportionment is the over- or underrepresentation (relative to population) of electoral districts.
  • It is a common feature of representative governments.
  • The Storting has 169 seats.
  • Seats are distributed by a formula: 1 point per resident, 1.8 points per unit of land.
  • Less populated rural districts with large tracts of land get disproportionately more seats than smaller, more populated urban districts.

Why this note matters

Land is weighted almost twice as heavily as people (1.8 vs. 1). That single ratio is the mechanism the correct answer has to name.

Why this note matters

This is the outcome of that formula, stated directly: rural districts are overrepresented. That's the fact that refutes "favors urban."

Dimmed notes are real but unused by the correct answer — true information, wrong relevance to this claim.

Answer choices

1 Less populated rural districts are disproportionally underrepresented, creating an unfair advantage for smaller but more populated urban districts. Reversed — the notes say rural districts are overrepresented, not underrepresented.
2 It's untrue that malapportionment favors small urban districts; rather, the formula overrepresents more populated districts. Reversed, more subtly — correctly says "untrue," but the notes say less populated districts are overrepresented, not more.
3 A common feature of representative governments, malapportionment occurs when electoral districts are over- or underrepresented. True, but off-target — accurate, straight from the notes, but never engages the actual claim being refuted.
4 Awarding more points per unit of land than points per resident, the formula for distributing Storting seats overrepresents less populated rural districts with large tracts of land. Correct — names the mechanism (land weighted over residents) and its consequence (rural overrepresentation), which refutes "favors urban."
Information and Ideas — Inferences R&W Q — Kuulo Kataa

If some artifacts recovered from excavations of the settlement of Kuulo Kataa date from the thirteenth century CE, that may lend credence to claims the settlement was founded around that time. There is other evidence, however, strongly supporting a fourteenth century CE founding date. If both are correct, that would imply that the artifacts originated elsewhere and eventually reached Kuulo Kataa through trade or migration.✓

the two facts the answer has to reconcile

What has to be true for both facts to hold

Artifacts predating the settlement, but the settlement founded later — the only way both survive is if the artifacts didn't originate at Kuulo Kataa. The answer must supply that reconciling mechanism, not just restate either fact.

Answer choices

1 fourteenth-century artifacts are more commonly recovered than thirteenth-century ones. Doesn't reconcile anything — a recovery-frequency comparison says nothing about why 13th-century artifacts exist at a 14th-century settlement.
2 the artifacts originated elsewhere and eventually reached Kuulo Kataa through trade or migration. Correct — the only option that lets both dates be true at once: the artifacts predate the settlement because they weren't made there.
3 Kuulo Kataa was founded by people from a different region than had previously been assumed. Unsupported leap — nothing in the premises supports a claim about who founded the settlement.
4 excavations may have inadvertently damaged some fourteenth-century artifacts. Off-target — damage during excavation doesn't explain a date discrepancy.
Information and Ideas — Inferences R&W Q — sea stars

Sea stars are thought to have evolved a headless layout from a known bilateral origin. Applying genomic markers from acorn worms to sea star body-patterning genes, researchers observed activity only in anterior genes across the entire body, and some posterior genes limited to the edges, suggesting that sea star morphology evolved to lack a trunk and consist primarily of a head region, rather than losing its head as previously assumed.✓

the two observations the answer must jointly explain prior belief being revised — not a trap, click it

Prior belief, not a trap

This is what scientists previously believed, stated so the finding can complicate it — it isn't bait pointing to a wrong answer, it's the premise the correct answer has to revise. That's a different job than a trap: a trap lures you toward a wrong choice, this tells you what the right choice needs to overturn.

What the two observations jointly say

"Head" genes active everywhere + "tail" genes barely present at the rim = the body reads as almost entirely head-type tissue. That's the reinterpretation the answer has to state.

Answer choices

1 posterior genes active at the edges are primarily responsible for the starlike layout. Overstates a minor finding — posterior genes were only "limited to the edges," not a primary driver.
2 sea stars did not evolve from a bilateral ancestor at all. Contradicts a given premise — the passage already states sea stars are "thought to have evolved... from a known bilateral origin."
3 there is only minimal correspondence in genetic markers between the two species. Unsupported by the finding — the genes were comparable enough to test, and clear activity patterns were found.
4 sea star morphology evolved to lack a trunk and consist primarily of a head region, rather than losing its head as previously assumed. Correct — matches both observations at once and explicitly overturns the stated assumption.
Craft and Structure — Words in Context R&W Q — committee report

Though the committee's initial report was measured in tone, its recommendations proved surprisingly sweeping✓: within a year, three departments had been dissolved and half the district's budget reallocated.

blank ↔ the evidence that defines it ‌trap — click it

The evidence defines the word

Dissolving three departments and reallocating half a budget are large, decisive actions — that's what the blank has to match. "Sweeping" (broad in scope, far-reaching) fits; a word implying caution or small scale wouldn't.

Trap — "measured in tone" describes delivery, not scope

"Measured" here means calm and restrained in how the report was written — it's easy to let that color your read of the blank and reach for "provisional" or "cautious." But the blank describes the recommendations' impact, which the colon evidence shows was anything but restrained.

Answer choices

1sweepingCorrect — matches the scale of the consequences described after the colon.
2redundantWrong — nothing in the evidence suggests duplication or repetition.
3provisionalWrong — tempting because of "measured in tone," but dissolving departments isn't a tentative action.
4ambiguousWrong — the consequences described are specific and decisive, not unclear.
Craft and Structure — Text Structure and Purpose R&W Q — brand trust

Economists have long assumed that consumer trust in a brand is built primarily through advertising. A 2019 study of over 400 companies, however, found that trust levels correlated far more strongly with employee reviews on public rating sites than with advertising spending. This suggests that a company's internal culture may shape public perception as much as, or more than, its marketing does.

setup — the assumption being complicated evidence — the complicating finding sentence in question

Setup

States the conventional assumption the paragraph is about to complicate — not itself the answer, but needed to see what the underlined sentence is doing in relation to.

Evidence

A specific study complicating the assumption above. This is the finding — the underlined sentence has to do something with this finding, not just repeat it.

What the underlined sentence is doing

It doesn't restate the setup (it says something new, not a rephrase of "advertising builds trust") and it doesn't describe methodology or a limitation (nothing here is about how the study was run or what it couldn't show). It takes the specific finding and generalizes it into a broader claim about internal culture — that's a conclusion drawn from evidence.

The underlined sentence primarily serves to —

1restate the initial assumption in different terms.Wrong — it contradicts the assumption (culture over advertising), it doesn't rephrase it.
2introduce a new methodology used by the 2019 study.Wrong — the study's methodology was already given in the prior sentence; this sentence doesn't describe method at all.
3draw a broader conclusion from the study's finding.Correct — generalizes the specific correlation into a claim about internal culture shaping perception.
4qualify a limitation of the study described earlier.Wrong — no limitation of the study is mentioned anywhere in the passage.
Craft and Structure — Cross-Text Connections R&W Q — UBI pilots

Text 1

Proponents of universal basic income (UBI) argue that a guaranteed monthly payment would reduce poverty without discouraging work, citing pilot programs in Finland and Kenya as evidence of its viability.

Text 2

Critics of UBI trials caution that most pilot programs have been too short in duration and too limited in scale to reliably predict how a full guaranteed-income policy would affect a national workforce over time.

the claim in Text 1 ↔ the specific rebuttal in Text 2

Same claim, linked across passages

Text 2 doesn't reject UBI outright, and it doesn't deny the pilots happened — it specifically targets the scope and duration Text 1 leans on as proof. The correct answer has to mirror that precise, narrow objection, not a broader disagreement Text 2 never makes.

Based on Text 2, how would its author most likely respond to the claim in Text 1?

1By agreeing that the pilot data is sufficient to guide national policy.Wrong — directly contradicts Text 2's stated caution about the pilots.
2By questioning whether the pilots' limited scope and duration support that conclusion.Correct — matches Text 2's specific objection, linked above.
3By denying that any UBI pilot programs have taken place.Wrong — Text 2 references the pilots as real; it questions their scale, not their existence.
4By arguing that UBI would necessarily increase poverty.Wrong — unsupported; Text 2 never makes a claim about poverty outcomes.

How should the "trap" phrase be marked?

Applied to rare manuscripts in Q14 — the plural noun that tempts a plural verb even though "collection" governs.

A — red squiggly (your suggestion)

The collection of rare manuscripts, donated by an anonymous alum last spring, ______ housed in the university's east wing.

Reads as "this is wrong" — same signal as a spellchecker. Risk: a student skimming might think the phrase itself is a grammar error, not that it's merely irrelevant to the verb.

B — dashed underline, warning tone, hover label recommended

The collection of rare manuscripts, donated by an anonymous alum last spring, ______ housed in the university's east wing.

Neutral amber, not red — "flagged," not "broken." Dashed (vs. solid) reads as provisional/annotative rather than an error mark. A hover tooltip spells out why without needing a click.

C — inline flag icon + label

The collection of ⚑rare manuscripts, donated by an anonymous alum last spring, ______ housed in the university's east wing.

Most explicit — good for a first-time tutorial pass over this question type, but adds visual noise if every question has 2-3 trap phrases. Better as an onboarding mode than the default.

D — hatch-pattern fill

The collection of rare manuscripts, donated by an anonymous alum last spring, ______ housed in the university's east wing.

Reads as "caution tape" — distinct from both the cue-color language and an error mark. Slightly heavier visually; works well at rest but competes if a cue color also touches this span.
